The product

One gesture, done well

Hold Fn and talk. A small pill above the Dock shows the app is listening. Let go, and within half a second the text appears in whatever app you're in. It's punctuated, the ums are gone, and "no wait, I meant Thursday" has already been applied. Your names and jargon are spelled right. Nothing leaves the Mac. When you fix a word after it's pasted, the app notices and gets it right next time.

That's the whole product. Everything in this document either makes that gesture faster, makes it more accurate, or makes it easier to install.

Why build it

Wispr proved the experience. Local models make it possible offline.

Wispr Flow

Excellent, but cloud-bound and heavy

Audio and on-screen context stream to Wispr's servers on every dictation, and history is uploaded. On this Mac it runs about 13 processes using roughly 1.35 GB of RAM, and keeps a 1.2 GB database plus a 1.1 GB daily backup.

Voicebox

Local, but bloated and fragile

It's a voice-cloning studio with dictation added on. A 485 MB Python server runs beside the app over localhost, with code for finding orphaned servers. Here it has 7 recordings on disk but 0 database rows, two identical 49-minute runaway recordings, and a crash right after them.

Meanwhile, local models have caught up. Parakeet TDT 0.6B transcribes at roughly 100 to 150 times real time on the Neural Engine, with about 2% word error on clean speech. Small open instruct models handle the cleanup. MLX can fine-tune them on the Mac itself.

The pill

Says what's happening, never steals focus

A small capsule above the Dock, in a panel that can't take focus. Listening: live waveform. Finishing: a brief shimmer, usually under half a second. Done: fades out. Paste not confirmed: "Copied. Press ⌘V". No speech: a small shake, nothing inserted.

Settings

Everything on one screen

Hotkey. Microphone (prefers the built-in mic over Bluetooth, to avoid low-quality headset audio). Language. Cleanup level: Off, Light or Polished. Learn from my edits. Launch at login. Keep audio for N days (default: never). Delete everything.

Dictionary

Words, aliases and replacements

Each entry has an optional "sounds like" alias, for example Northwind ← "kai gen". Entries you add, entries auto-learned from your corrections (tagged as such), and an opt-in import of contact names. Replacements like "my email" → your address.

The Fn key

Handled during setup

If macOS is set to open the emoji picker on 🌐, setup shows you how to switch that off, or offers Right Option as the hold key instead. All hotkeys can be rebound.

Speed

Do the work while you talk

Your Wispr dictations average 33 seconds. No local model cleans 33 seconds of speech in half a second if it starts from scratch on release. So we don't start on release. Recognition runs on the Neural Engine and cleanup on the GPU, so they work side by side while you're still speaking. Releasing the key leaves only the last few seconds to finish.

Skip gate

Short and clean? Skip the model.

Under about 8 words, with nothing for the rules to fix, the rules output goes straight in. Quick replies land in under 150 ms.

Copy-heavy decoding

Cleanup mostly repeats its input

That suits prompt-lookup speculative decoding, which drafts tokens from the input itself. Expect roughly 2–3× faster generation, to be verified in M0.

Two destinations

Names go to recognition, style goes to cleanup

Spellings are best fixed where the words are heard, so names from the screen, your dictionary and conversation participants go to Parakeet's keyword spotter. They cost the cleanup model nothing. The cleanup prompt only gets what changes the writing: app style, the text before the cursor, the selection, your name and the time.

Token budget

About 300 tokens of context, at most

The cleanup model reads every context token before it writes a word, so a 2,000-token screen dump would add hundreds of milliseconds to each dictation. We cap dynamic context at about 300 tokens, cache the fixed system prompt, and measure what each piece costs in M0.

Architecture

One Swift process, nothing else

You asked for Rust or something like it. For a Mac-only app, Swift is the better fit and just as efficient: compiled, no garbage collector, no runtime, no webview. More importantly, every piece that matters here is Apple-native, and Swift reaches it without a bridge.

NeedSwiftRust
Parakeet on the Neural EngineFluidAudio (CoreML), used by VoiceInk, Spokenly and HexSwift bridge needed, or Metal via transcribe.cpp: no Neural Engine, no documented word biasing
LLM inference and LoRA trainingMLX Swift, Apple's own, including LoRA trainingLess mature bindings
Event tap, Accessibility, lazy clipboard, floating panelFirst-class APIsobjc2 FFI throughout; rdev is reported to crash on macOS 14+ off the main thread
Apple Foundation Models, SpeechAnalyzerDirectSwift bridge (Handy does exactly this)
UISwiftUI and AppKit, a few MBTauri: a webview plus a JS frontend, the weight we're avoiding
Decision: Swift 6, SwiftUI for the two small windows, AppKit for the pill, one process, no Dock icon. If Windows ever matters, revisit with Rust and transcribe.cpp.

The cleanup pipeline

  • Rules, always, under 5 ms. Dictionary aliases, filler removal, spacing and capitals, spoken punctuation, numbers, emails and URLs.
  • Skip gate. Short and already clean goes straight to paste.
  • Model. A fixed system prompt with cached KV, the context snapshot, the app's style, 3–5 of your own past corrections, and the raw text. It outputs only the cleaned text.
  • Validator. Rejects output that changes length too much, adds content (answers a question instead of transcribing it) or misses a 1.5 s deadline, and falls back to the rules output. The row records which path won.

Two models: a shipped base, and a personal one per user

Personal history never goes into the model other people download.

  • Base model (ships with the app). Qwen3.5-0.8B fine-tuned on data that isn't anyone's private history. Built with the same generators as Kai's v2:
    • self-corrections ("Tuesday, sorry, Wednesday" becomes "Wednesday"), spoken lists, emails with greeting and sign-off, "quote… unquote", continuations across chunks;
    • filled with public text, plus messy spoken versions written by a large open model (for example Qwen 27B);
    • public conversational speech for realistic hesitations;
    • tested on dictations from consenting volunteers who aren't Kai.
    Downloaded at setup (~430 MB).
  • Personal model (on each Mac). A small adapter trained in the background on that user's own data:
    • their dictionary and on-screen names, from day one;
    • their edits;
    • optionally, the one-tap Wispr import.
    It goes live only after it beats the base model on that user's own held-out dictations.
  • Kai's model today is a personal model: trained on Kai's Wispr history (10,429 examples in v2, including 994 real self-corrections). It stays on Kai's Mac.

The prompt carries a short context header (app, type, vocabulary, text already written), so one model covers every app and style.

Learning

Four layers, from instant to overnight

This is Wispr's improvement loop, at the scale of one person and entirely on the Mac. The signal is what you change after a paste. After inserting, the app re-reads the field when focus leaves it, after 30 seconds and after 2 minutes, and compares only the span it inserted. Fields it can't read give no signal and are skipped; it never guesses.

Immediately

Dictionary auto-learn

A one-word fix that sounds like what was heard becomes a dictionary entry with an alias, tagged Auto-learned. It's dropped if unused for 30 days.

signal: the edit
Next dictation

Recognition biasing

Dictionary terms steer Parakeet's keyword spotter, chosen by relevance to the current app and window, such as Slack channel members.

signal: dictionary + context
Next dictation

Your own examples

The 3–5 most similar past dictations from the same app, with your final text, go into the cleanup prompt.

signal: accepted and edited rows
Nightly, on power

Personal adapter

Once there are 200+ new rows: LoRA training on raw → final, then DPO with your edit as preferred and our output as rejected.

signal: edits as preferences
The shadow gate, taken from Wispr

No adapter goes live untested

Each candidate is scored on a held-out 10% of your edited dictations, by how close it gets to your final text. It goes live only if it beats the current adapter and doesn't regress on the fixed golden set. The previous adapter stays available for one-click rollback. Every row records the model and adapter that produced it, so progress shows week by week.

On reinforcement learning

Preference learning first, RL when there's data

At one person's scale, DPO on your edits is the stable, sample-efficient form. The reward is plain: make you edit less. Once there are thousands of rows, GRPO with reward equal to negative edit distance uses the same data and the same gate. Wispr uses GRPO for Canto across its whole user base. Per-user speech model training is out of scope; biasing gets most of that benefit with far less risk.

Three uses

  • Recognition eval with real audio. Run Parakeet and the alternatives on the 846 clips and score them against your edited text: a word error rate on your own voice, accent and vocabulary.
  • Cleanup head-to-head. Feed Wispr's raw recognition into our cleaner and compare with your final text. This compares directly against Wispr's formatting stage.
  • Training seed for the base cleanup model. Everything stays on your machine.

Privacy

Nothing leaves the Mac

  • Network: model downloads and update checks only, to fixed hosts. Settings says so in one plain line.
  • No telemetry by default. Crash reports are opt-in.
  • Password fields are detected and never recorded.
  • No audio kept by default. Text history stays in the app's own folder. "Delete everything" removes history, adapters and the dictionary.
  • Hardened runtime, notarized, with the smallest set of entitlements.

Risks and questions

What could go wrong, and what we'd do

RiskResponse
The cleanup model is too slow on a base M1 for long dictations.Clean while you talk, prompt-lookup decoding, the 0.8B model on low-end Macs, and a 1.5 s deadline with rules fallback.

Open questions for you

  1. Does Wispr keep any audio? Yes: 846 clips, 7 hours, in a roughly two-week rolling window. See Your Wispr data.
